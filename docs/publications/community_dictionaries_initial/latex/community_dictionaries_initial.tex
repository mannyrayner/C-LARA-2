\begin{abstract}
Community Dictionaries is a lightweight web application in which small groups
construct picture and audio dictionaries together, using phone photographs,
human recordings and optional written information. It is designed to support
languages independently of whether AI language services are available.
Construction and discussion take priority over dedicated practice activities.
The initial implementation shares the accounts and server infrastructure of
C-LARA-2 but has its own data model, permissions and private media storage.
Development proceeded through two substantial AI implementation iterations,
with human specification, installation and feedback. By 25 September 2026,
Manny reported successful laptop use on the AWS deployment and a successful
trial on Cathy's phone: she took and saved a photograph, and he added a recording.
We describe the implemented system, the evidence behind this result, and the
questions for a proposed Icelandic pilot. These are initial feasibility results,
not evidence of learning effectiveness or comprehensive mobile compatibility.
\end{abstract}

\section{Purpose and scope}

The starting question was practical: could a small language-learning community
have a useful tool for collecting, discussing and improving picture and audio
entries without a large software-development project? Indigenous and other
under-resourced languages are a central motivation. However, the application
also supports languages for which AI assistance is readily available. This
allows an initial Swedish trial, whose linguistic content Manny can assess
confidently, without making AI-generated content a prerequisite.

The first version deliberately concentrates on \emph{constructing and
discussing}. Someone supplies a photograph, another person supplies the word or
phrase as a recording, and the group can clarify or improve the contribution.
Browsing and listening are already possible. Quizzes, spaced repetition and
other dedicated practice activities are deferred until there is useful material
and a satisfactory contribution workflow. Whether the construction activity
itself supports learning is an empirical question.

Phone camera and microphone input are primary modalities. Written forms,
translations and categories are optional, so an entry need not wait for a
spelling or a typed explanation. The initial implementation makes no calls to
AI content-generation services and requires no AI-provider credentials for
dictionary work. Future text-to-speech and image generation are possible
additions, not assumptions underlying the current design.

\section{What participants can do}

A dictionary has a target language, an optional language for explanations, and
an invited membership. Members can create entries, contribute media, discuss
entries, and propose changes. The owner and editors manage review. An entry
can start with only a picture or a recording; incomplete contributions are a
normal part of the workflow, rather than an error condition.

For example, a learner might photograph an object and ask a partner for its
name. The partner adds a recording; either person can add a written form,
translation or category. The group can discuss the intended meaning through
written or recorded comments. Several recordings or pictures can be retained
with their contributor attribution. Accepted wording and the selected picture
provide a simple presentation while preserving the contribution history.
Ordinary members' proposals go through review; owners and editors can accept
material or request changes. Written revisions are versioned to avoid silently
overwriting a newer accepted form.

The interface supports image browsing, listening, text search and category
filtering. Text search uses the written information that participants have
supplied; it is not automatic recognition of objects or speech. Picture and
recording uploads supplement direct camera and microphone capture. The
recorder includes input-device selection, a microphone check and level meter,
preview, and detection of silent takes where the browser permits it.

Named \emph{partnerships} organise collaboration within a dictionary. A request
for a missing picture or recording can be addressed to a partnership, whose
members can retrieve outstanding requests and respond. This makes a division
of labour explicit: contributors need not provide every component themselves.
Requests remain distinguishable as open, awaiting review, completed or withdrawn.

\textbf{Privacy follows dictionary membership.} A partnership is a work group,
not a separate privacy boundary. People who want material visible only to a
subgroup should create a separate dictionary. Media access is checked against
membership and the files are stored outside the web server's public media
directory. This is application-level access control, not end-to-end encryption;
server operators retain technical access. An owner can export records and media,
but the export is not yet a one-click restoration facility.

\section{A small addition to an existing platform}

Community Dictionaries is a separate Django application in the C-LARA-2
repository, available at
\url{https://c-lara-2.c-lara.org/community-dictionaries/}.
It reuses existing accounts, database infrastructure and deployment services.
It does not invoke C-LARA-2's text-compilation pipeline or task queue, and its
entries are separate from the older project-linked picture dictionaries.
This boundary permits immediate shared deployment and leaves open a later
move to an independent repository.

The interface uses Django templates and a small amount of JavaScript and CSS,
without a separate frontend build. Browser facilities provide recording and
local draft recovery. Submission receipts make interrupted uploads safely
retryable without intentionally duplicating the contribution. Images are
validated and re-encoded; audio is stored in supported browser/upload formats
without server-side transcoding. Authenticated media responses support the
range requests used by audio players. These choices keep the service small,
while leaving device-specific media compatibility as an explicit test concern.

The shared AWS installation uses nginx, Gunicorn and PostgreSQL. Code deployment
does not transfer a laptop's dictionary database or recordings to that server.
Operational backups must cover the shared database and the private dictionary
files as well as the platform's existing media.

\section{Development process and early evidence}

The development experiment asks whether a capable AI assistant can produce and
maintain a useful, bounded application in a few substantial iterations. Here,
the assistant wrote the implementation, tests and supporting documentation;
Manny supplied goals, design decisions, installation work and trial feedback.
There was no separate handoff to a delegated coding agent. Each iteration
contained multiple tool calls, tests and repairs: ``a few iterations'' does not
mean a few model invocations.

The first implementation already supported dictionary creation, image and
audio uploads, browsing, collaboration and review. Human testing identified
two immediately useful improvements. Optional written information was present
but difficult to discover, so the fields and editing actions were made more
visible. Direct recording produced silence on Manny's laptop, prompting better
microphone selection and signal feedback. Manny subsequently identified the
wrong default input device and confirmed recording with the correct one.
The evidence does not establish that the app caused the original device issue.

Manny estimated the first functional implementation at about 45 minutes and
the initial implementation and repair work at under two hours. These are
participant estimates, not instrumented timing measurements. They do not
establish the total cost of earlier discussion, existing infrastructure,
human installation, testing or subsequent maintenance. The development
conversation refers to the assistant as GPT-6 Astra; the iteration reports do
not independently record its serving model identifier, token usage or monetary cost.

The phone result confirms an actual contribution workflow on one physical

need investigation. Existing technical tests and the dated development records

On 26 September, Manny also confirmed that the deployed Django settings had
\texttt{DEBUG = False}. The deployment check still reported warnings about
secure cookies, HTTPS redirection and HTTP Strict Transport Security. These
are outstanding configuration items to reconcile with nginx, not evidence
that the successful functional trials did not occur. The prototype should not
be presented as having completed a production security review.

\section{The next trial and its questions}

Kate and Axel have been invited to try an Icelandic dictionary together; no
outcome from that trial is yet available. A suitable first task is to create a
few entries about objects or situations they encounter, sharing photography,
recording and explanation as they find natural. The important question is
whether the activity becomes worth returning to, beyond whether each button
works. Initial feedback should address:
\begin{itemize}
\item where either participant hesitates, needs help or abandons an action;
\item whether requests, comments and review support their actual collaboration;
\item whether the resulting entries are useful, and whether they voluntarily return to add or consult material.
\end{itemize}

Device/browser details and any failed capture or playback should accompany
technical feedback. The first unfamiliar-user trial should also check whether
account access, membership and the distinction between partnerships and private
dictionaries are understandable without continuous assistance.

Short video clips may help illustrate actions, and TTS or image generation may
be useful in languages where those services work well. All remain deferred,
along with dedicated practice activities. An Indigenous-language trial would
add questions that Swedish and Icelandic testing cannot settle: community
authority, consent for images and voices, culturally restricted material,
hosting and data governance, and the suitability of the contribution and
review model. Avoiding AI service calls does not by itself settle those matters.

\section{What this first version establishes}

We have a working implementation and initial human evidence from a shared
server and a physical phone. The combination of small scope, reused
infrastructure, AI implementation and short human feedback cycles has produced
a result suitable for a more informative user trial. It supports the feasibility
of this development approach for this application; it does not yet establish
learning gains, long-term maintainability or a general development-time bound.
The next evidence should come from people choosing to use the tool repeatedly
and from our ability to respond reliably to what they discover.
