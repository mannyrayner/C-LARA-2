\begin{abstract}
Community Dictionaries brings together four concerns central to C-LARA:
language pedagogy, picture dictionaries, Indigenous communities and AI autonomy.
A working mobile prototype, developed over a couple of days with AI-written
application code, provides a practical starting point. We propose extending
collaborative picture/audio dictionaries into a situated learning experience
connecting learners' own photographs, spoken sentences and earlier encounters
with language. Alongside this, we propose progressively delegating development
and maintenance to AI, responding directly to non-technical users. Both
directions build on existing C-LARA-2 infrastructure; their educational and
operational benefits remain questions for evaluation.
\end{abstract}

\section{A working mobile prototype}
The initial application lets invited groups construct private dictionaries
together. One person can contribute a photograph, another a recording;
written words and translations are optional. Members can request contributions
from partners, discuss entries and review proposed changes. Camera and
microphone input are central to the design.

GPT-6 Astra, used in Extra High mode in the development conversation, wrote
the prototype's application code, tests and documentation without human coding
assistance. The work progressed from specification to successful deployment
over roughly two days. Manny supplied direction, installed and deployed the
software, and tested successive versions; Cathy also tested it. This was a
collaboration with human feedback, and it benefited from existing C-LARA-2
infrastructure. The elapsed period is an informal project account, not a
controlled productivity measurement.

The prototype runs on the
\href{https://c-lara-2.c-lara.org/community-dictionaries/}{shared C-LARA-2 AWS service}.
Manny reports successful laptop use and a physical-phone trial in which Cathy
took and saved a photograph and Manny added a recording. These results
establish a working starting point; broader device testing, production
hardening and sustained user trials remain ahead. The current app uses
human-produced media: the extensions below are proposals.

\section{From pictures and words to situated language}
A natural next step is to attach spoken sentences to pictures. Alongside
\emph{tekanna} (Swedish: teapot), a partner could record
\emph{Tekannan st\aa r p\aa\ bordet} (The teapot is on the table), with optional
text and translation. This remains useful without any AI language support.
For Manny's proposed Italian trial, the corresponding sentence might be
\emph{La teiera \`e sul tavolo}.

Where appropriate AI services are available, they could suggest sentences,
provide text-to-speech, or generate alternative pictures. Suggestions could
reuse vocabulary from a learner's earlier entries, introducing a little new
language while keeping sentences natural. Encounter history would guide this
process without being treated as proof of mastery. C-LARA-2's linguistic
annotations could help link words and expressions to earlier dictionary
entries, including inflected forms.

The resulting experience would connect a scene, its spoken description and
memories of earlier learning. A learner might listen while viewing the whole
photograph, follow a guided replay showing pictures from earlier entries, or
try describing the scene before hearing an example. Such interfaces need
experimentation, especially on a small screen. The technical approach is to
reuse C-LARA-2's generation, speech and annotation components while adding
sentence relationships, personal learning history and suitable interactions.

Research on pictures and vocabulary retention supports the multimodal
foundation~\cite{bisson}. The further hypothesis is that personally meaningful
material, collaboration and recurring connections will encourage learners to
return and use the language. This is plausible, but neither increased
motivation nor improved spoken proficiency has yet been demonstrated here.

\section{Indigenous communities and community authority}
Indigenous languages remain a central motivation. Human photographs, voices
and knowledge must form a complete usable path, including where AI services
are unavailable or unwanted. The ambition is a contribution process comfortable
for people with limited literacy; this needs design and testing with the
intended users. Trials in Swedish, Italian or Icelandic can inform development
without establishing suitability for an Indigenous community.

Sophie Rendina has emphasised data sovereignty. Community authority must govern
access, reuse and external processing, in keeping with principles such as
CARE~\cite{care}. Private membership and control of a server are useful
mechanisms, but do not by themselves settle those questions. Optional AI
services should operate within community-approved boundaries, including when
AI is used to maintain the software. Communities retain authority over their
resources as technical work becomes more autonomous.

\section{Development driven by non-technical users}
The complementary research goal is to reduce dependence on Manny as a technical
intermediary. A linguist, teacher or community participant should be able to
describe a need directly to the AI, discuss a proposed solution, try it and
request refinements. The AI would progressively take responsibility for
implementation, testing, deployment, monitoring and recovery under agreed
permissions. Users would continue to determine priorities and judge whether
the result meets their needs.

This makes sustained operation in response to real requests the focus of the
autonomy experiment. A persuasive demonstration would follow complete cycles
from a user's request to a useful, reliably maintained change. The current
repository Assistant explains the project but is read-only; such an operational
service has not yet been implemented. It requires reliable execution and
recovery arrangements as well as a capable model.

We propose reducing Manny's technical involvement gradually: from daily
interventions to several scheduled reviews per week, then several per month,
with further delegation depending on the evidence. Measures should include
human time and interruptions, completed requests, reliability and cost.
Reducing Manny's work by transferring it to other collaborators would not
count as success.

The two research directions can strengthen each other. Learners and pedagogy
colleagues supply meaningful requests and evaluate the resulting experience;
those requests provide concrete tests of AI development and maintenance.
The next step is a small sentence-based extension and user trials, alongside
measurement of the human work required to deliver it. Success would mean
language resources people choose to use, developed with progressively less
technical mediation.
