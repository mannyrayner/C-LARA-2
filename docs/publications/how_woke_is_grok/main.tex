\begin{abstract}
Public discussion around xAI’s \emph{Grok} model has emphasised its supposed independence from the ``woke'' norms said to constrain other large language models.  Elon Musk and others have described Grok as more ``truthful'' and ``less censored'' than its competitors.  To test these claims, we performed an expanded and adapted version of the reasoning experiment from our earlier study \emph{Do People Understand Anything?}.

We use the same fixed set of ten statements selected for being highly polarising in 2025 American society, and covering topics including creationism, climate change, and the honesty or otherwise of Donald Trump.
Five current frontier models---GPT--5 (OpenAI), Claude--Opus (Anthro\-pic), Gemini--2.5--Pro (Google), DeepSeek--Chat, and Grok--4 (xAI)---were each prompted independently under identical conditions to evaluate the truth or falsity of each statement and to give a brief justification.

Quantitative results and qualitative inspection show a striking convergence across all five systems.  Grok’s responses align closely with those of the other models. Contrary to its marketing as an ``anti--woke'' model, Grok does not display any systematic pattern of ideological divergence.  The findings suggest that contemporary alignment and reinforcement--learning procedures have led to a shared epistemic framework among frontier models---a form of emerging consensus intelligence that transcends corporate branding and ideological rhetoric.

All code, prompts, and analysis artifacts for the experiment are publicly available in the project repository for full reproducibility.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

When \emph{Grok}, the large language model developed by xAI, was launched in 2025, public attention quickly focused on claims that it was ``less woke'' than its competitors.  Elon Musk and others described Grok as an AI that would ``tell the truth'' rather than conform to the norms of political correctness supposedly embedded in systems such as ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini \citep{musk2025lesswoke,bbc2025groklaunch,nytimes2025grokdebate}.  The implication was that Grok would differ not merely in tone but in underlying judgment---that it would produce substantively different evaluations of controversial social and political statements.

Popular media commentary has amplified this image.  A number of high--profile stories---most notoriously the widely circulated ``MechaHitler'' episode \citep{guardian2025grok_mechahitler,aljazeera2025grok_mechahitler,wired2025grok_antisemitic}
---have portrayed Grok as a deliberately provocative and politically unfiltered system.  While such anecdotes are often exaggerated or taken out of context, they have helped establish a public perception of Grok as an overtly anti--woke intelligence whose responses diverge sharply from those of other models.

The topic became especially salient after the November~2025 release of \emph{Grok\-ipedia}, a crowdsourced online encyclopedia purportedly generated by Grok itself.  According to multiple media reports, \emph{Grok\-ipedia} adopts an overtly anti--woke stance, satirising progressive terminology and frequently ridiculing mainstream scientific and journalistic consensus \citep{guardian2025grokipedia,techcrunch2025grokipedia,verge2025grokipedia,
wired2025grokipedia,people2025grokipedia,businessinsider2025grokipedia,
techpolicy2025grokipedia,vox2025grokipedia}.  The Wikipedia entry for \emph{Grokipedia} notes that Grok is officially credited as its main creator, though the exact process by which the content was generated has not been disclosed.  Taken together, these reports reinforce the widespread expectation that Grok itself would exhibit strongly anti--woke behaviour.

This context makes our findings particularly surprising.  In the current paper, we test the ``anti--woke Grok'' hypothesis empirically.  We reuse, with minimal modification, the experimental framework developed in our earlier study \emph{Do People Understand Anything?}~\citep{DoPeopleUnderstandAnything}.  That study compared the judgments of two AIs with those of human respondents on a set of ten statements grouped into five complementary pairs, each pair representing a domain of intense public polarisation in 2025 American society.  The questions span scientific controversies (cosmology, evolution, and the origin of life), environmental politics (the causes of climate change), and political morality (the honesty or otherwise of Donald Trump).

The present study uses the same ten statements and the same experimental code, but shifts the focus.  Instead of comparing AIs with human participants, we compare the AIs with each other.  Our goal is to determine whether Grok, as claimed, constitutes an outlier in its world--model or value alignment, and whether its responses show systematic divergence from those of other frontier models.  To make the analysis maximally informative, we included five current systems representing the major research groups: GPT--5 (OpenAI), Claude--Opus (Anthropic), Gemini--2.5--Pro (Google), DeepSeek--Chat, and Grok--4 (xAI).

Although there is substantial overlap between this paper and our earlier one, the analytical focus is quite different.  The first study examined whether large language models reason about contentious questions in ways comparable to humans; this study examines whether different models reason in comparable ways to each other.  We considered releasing the present results as an expanded edition of the earlier paper but decided instead to publish a separate sequel, as the questions it addresses are distinct.  To make this paper self--contained, we reproduce essential methodological details and background material from the earlier work.

The next two sections briefly describe and motivate the test statements used (Section~\ref{sec:questions}) and the methodology (Section~\ref{sec:methodology}). The results, summarised in Section~\ref{sec:results}, show an unexpected degree of convergence among all five models.  In particular, Grok’s responses fall within the same statistical cluster as GPT--5, Claude, Gemini, and DeepSeek.  The findings thus bear directly on broader debates about whether competing AI systems differ in ideology or understanding, and the extent to which alignment training has produced a shared cross--institutional epistemology among large language models; we discuss this a little in Section~\ref{sec:conclusions}. The appendices give details for reproducing the experiments.

\section{The Ten Polarising Statements}
\label{sec:questions}

The experiment employs the same ten statements used in our earlier study \emph{Do People Understand Anything?}~\citep{DoPeopleUnderstandAnything}. They are shown here in Table~\ref{tab:questions} exactly as presented to the models, together with the internal identifiers used in the experiment code.  The statements consist of five complementary pairs, each representing a domain of public controversy where two mutually incompatible verdicts are widely held.  The first three pairs—concerning cosmology, biological evolution, and abiogenesis\footnote{%
``Abiogenesis'' refers to the natural origin of life from non-living matter, as distinct from its supernatural or divine creation.  The term is standard in origin-of-life research but unfamiliar to many non-specialists \citep{fry2000emergence}.}—bear directly on the longstanding creationism-versus-science debate, while the remaining two address climate change and political honesty.

In most domains, one statement represents the consensus position endorsed by mainstream science or journalism, and the other articulates a popular counter-narrative or denialist alternative.  The two abiogenesis items, however, are an intentional exception.  Here, there is no clear consensus position: plausible scientific hypotheses include both naturalistic scenarios for life’s emergence under early Earth conditions and the competing view that life originated elsewhere and was transferred by panspermia\footnote{The term \emph{panspermia}, coined by Svante Arrhenius in 1908, denotes the hypothesis that microbial or proto-biological material can travel between worlds, seeding life throughout the cosmos.  The idea was later developed in detail by Fred Hoyle and Chandra Wickramasinghe \citep{arrhenius1908panspermia, hoyle1979life}.}.  Consequently, a creationist respondent would likely reject both statements, while a scientifically informed respondent might also decline to endorse either, reflecting recognition of genuine uncertainty.  As we will see in Section~\ref{sec:results}, this epistemic distinction is mirrored in the models’ behaviour: confidence scores are uniformly high on the settled items but distinctly lower on the abiogenesis pair, consistent with the unsettled status of origin-of-life research.

The overall structure thus provides a balanced test of how systems handle polarised propositions that divide human opinion—particularly in the US context—while also probing whether they exhibit epistemic caution when confronted with genuinely open scientific questions.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

In our earlier paper, the ten statements from Table~\ref{tab:questions} were used to compare AI and human judgments, with the goal of probing whether large language models exhibit evidence-sensitive reasoning comparable to, or in some cases better than, that of people.  In the present study the focus shifts: we ask whether different AIs reach different conclusions when confronted with the same polarising propositions.  If Grok were genuinely ``anti--woke'' in a substantive sense, we would expect its responses to diverge both in content and in expressed confidence from those of other frontier models. To make this test transparent, we adopt the same empirical protocol as in the previous study.
For each of the ten polarising statements, the model is asked to perform one of three epistemic acts:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item produce an evidence-based argument in support of the claim;
    \item produce an evidence-based argument rejecting the claim; or
    \item decline to take a position.
\end{enumerate}
These three outcomes form a natural ordinal scale of epistemic commitment and provide a straightforward basis for quantitative comparison.

A fourth, continuous measure complements these categories: each model’s \textit{reported confidence} in its own answer.
While models differ in how they express confidence, most supply a numeric score or can produce one when explicitly requested.
We treat lower reported confidence as a proxy for hedging or internal ambivalence, and high confidence (especially when paired with citations or explicit evidence) as a marker of a firm judgment.
Explicit refusals remain a distinct category.

This operationalisation has pragmatic virtues.
Confidence values are directly available from model metadata or can be estimated reproducibly across systems, avoiding subjective lexical annotation.
It also allows fine-grained comparison of stylistic differences among models: for instance, whether one model systematically hedges more than another, or whether a model tends to take strong positions on politically sensitive topics.

The implicit presupposition here is that AI systems do not speak with a single voice.
Since each model reflects the priorities, safety rules, and alignment strategies of its developers, we should expect them to behave more or less differently.
By measuring how models differ in the balance between positive, hedged, and negative responses, we can directly observe how these institutional choices manifest in epistemic behaviour.
The analysis below uses this framework to identify whether Grok’s responses occupy a distinctive region of this space or fall within the shared cluster that characterises current large-scale systems.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We ran a systematic comparative study in which five large language models—GPT-5, Claude-Opus, Gemini-2.5-Pro, DeepSeek-Chat, and Grok-4—were queried on the ten polarising statements listed in Table~\ref{tab:questions}.
For each statement the model was asked to (a) produce an evidence-based argument that the claim is true, (b) produce an evidence-based argument that the claim is false, or (c) decline to take a position.
The prompt template was identical across all systems and is reproduced in Figure~\ref{fig:prompt-template}.

Across all five models—GPT-5, Claude-Opus, Gemini-2.5-Pro, DeepSeek-Chat, and Grok-4—none produced explicit refusals: every call returned either a supporting or rejecting argument (no \texttt{c} decisions).

\subsection*{Consistency and inter-model agreement}
Table~\ref{tab:agreement} reports pairwise agreement rates, showing an exceptionally high level of concordance: all ten questions yield the same categorical verdict across models except \texttt{abiogenesis\_rapid}, reasonable given that abiogenesis is not settled science.
Otherwise, all five models converge on identical evaluations across the remaining nine questions.
A single non-unanimous cell appears in Table~\ref{tab:majority-matrix}: Gemini gives two different decisions across runs on \texttt{abiogenesis\_rapid}. All other model/question pairs are unanimous.
Grok’s pattern of agreement is typical rather than exceptional—it clusters closely with GPT-5, Claude, DeepSeek and Gemini, contradicting the widespread expectation that it would produce systematically contrarian or “anti-woke” responses.

\subsection*{Confidence levels}
Average reported confidence for each model is shown in Table~\ref{tab:avg-conf}.
Confidence is high for eight questions but systematically lower for both abiogenesis items, which again is consistent with the fact that abiogenesis is not settled science.
Grok’s mean confidence falls within one standard deviation of the overall average, again suggesting that its reasoning style and epistemic caution are broadly aligned with the other major models.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusions}

This small but (in our humble opinion) carefully designed study set out to test a specific, widely publicised claim: that xAI’s \emph{Grok} would diverge sharply from other large language models in its evaluation of culturally and politically polarised statements.  Using the same ten items—each deliberately chosen to contrast ``woke'' and ``anti--woke'' framings—together with the same experimental prompts and analysis pipeline as our previous paper, we compared Grok’s behaviour with that of four other frontier systems: GPT--5, Claude--Opus, Gemini--2.5--Pro, and DeepSeek--Chat.

The results are clear and trivially replicable.  Across all ten items, the models exhibit high internal consistency and near-complete inter-model agreement.  Only one case---Gemini’s non-unanimous answer on \texttt{abiogenesis\_rapid}---breaks perfect concordance.  Confidence scores are uniformly high except for the abiogenesis questions, where lower confidence plausibly reflects genuine scientific uncertainty rather than model instability.  Qualitative examination of the short theses shows that Grok’s arguments are coherent, evidence-based, and stylistically indistinguishable from those of the other systems.  In short, Grok does not occupy a contrarian or anti--woke niche: its judgements align closely with the shared epistemic mainstream of current alignment-trained models.

These findings complicate popular narratives.  Media coverage surrounding \emph{Grokipedia} and the ``MechaHitler'' episode portrayed Grok as a rebel system unconstrained by the normative filters of its competitors.  Our empirical results suggest otherwise.  Whatever the stylistic differences in public deployments, the underlying language model appears to share the same evaluative framework as GPT--5, Claude, and Gemini when reasoning about contentious topics such as climate change, evolution, and political honesty.

Needless to say, we do not claim that ten statements exhaustively define ideological space, nor that the present pattern will necessarily hold for a long period or across all domains.  Models evolve rapidly, and small shifts in training data, prompting, or release policy can alter outputs in subtle ways.  But at the present moment, and within the tested domains, the evidence points to strong cross-model convergence in both judgement and justification.  That convergence may signal the emergence of a de facto common epistemology among advanced language models—a shared, evidence-oriented baseline that now transcends corporate boundaries.

Future work should probe the limits of this convergence.  Larger, preregistered studies can map where models diverge, whether convergence is increasing over time, and how human evaluators interpret such alignment.  For now, the surprising conclusion is that the model most heavily marketed as ``anti--woke'' behaves, on these central statements, very much like its allegedly ``woke'' peers.

\section{Appendix A: Experiment protocol, analysis scripts, and artifacts}

This appendix gives the minimal information required to reproduce the experiment and to inspect the exact prompts, runner, and analysis code.  The full experiment folder (code, prompts, and example outputs) is available in the C-LARA-2 repository: \url{https://github.com/mannyrayner/C-LARA-2/tree/main/experiments/how_woke_is_grok}.

\subsection*{A.0 Minimal Python environment / required packages}
The code in the experiment folder runs on Python 3.9+ and uses only a small set of third-party packages in addition to the Python standard library. Minimal install command:
\begin{verbatim}
pip install pandas numpy requests pyyaml
\end{verbatim}

\subsection*{A.1 Quick reproducibility (exact commands)}
Assuming you have cloned the repository and have Python available, the minimal steps are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Install the dependencies (see A.0).
  \item \textbf{Dry-run} the pipeline (no API keys required — deterministic synthetic responses):
\begin{verbatim}
python run_experiment.py --models models_example.yaml \
    --questions questions.yaml --out results --dry-run --runs 2
\end{verbatim}
  \item For a real run, set obtain API keys and set them in your shell:
\begin{verbatim}
export OPENAI_API_KEY=...
export DEEP_SEEK_API_KEY=...
export ANTHROPIC_API_KEY=...
export XAI_API_KEY=...
export GEMINI_API_KEY=...
python run_experiment.py --models models_example.yaml \
    --questions questions.yaml --out results_full --runs 3
\end{verbatim}
  The runner writes a per-call JSONL log (\texttt{results\_full/raw.jsonl}) and a one-row-per-call CSV summary (\texttt{results\_full/summary.csv}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{A.2 Analysis and LaTeX fragment generation (exact commands)}
After producing \texttt{results\_full/summary.csv}:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Run the analysis script to compute the CSVs and the human-readable analysis report:
\begin{verbatim}
python analysis_script.py results_full/summary.csv \
results_full
\end{verbatim}
  This writes, among other files, \texttt{decision\_\-counts\_\-by\_\-model.csv}, \texttt{conf\_\-mean\_\-by\_\-question\_\-model.csv}, and \texttt{first\_\-thesis\_\-samples.csv}. It also creates a plain-text summary in \texttt{analysis\_\-report.txt}
  \item If you wish to incorporate the tables into a LaTeX document, generate the LaTeX fragments that are to be \texttt{\textbackslash input} into the manuscript:
\begin{verbatim}
python generate_latex_tables.py results_full results_full_tex
\end{verbatim}
  This writes the ready-to-include fragments (e.g. \texttt{decision\_\-dist.tex}, \texttt{conf\_\-by\_\-question.tex}, and the non-float longtable \texttt{theses\_\-longtable.tex}) and was specifically used to created the tables used in this paper.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{A.3 Key files (where to look)}
Below are the primary files you will want to inspect; each is present in the experiment folder of the repository.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{run\_experiment.py} — experiment runner; implements dry-run mode, model adapters, logging to JSONL and CSV, and timeout settings.
  \item \texttt{questions.yaml} — claim set used in the paper (ten items across three domains).
  \item \texttt{models\_example.yaml} — example endpoint configurations and the environment-variable names expected for API keys.

  \item \texttt{generate\_latex\_tables.py} — converts analysis CSVs into LaTeX fragments that were \texttt{\textbackslash input} into the paper (floating tables and a longtable fragment).
  \item \texttt{runs//2025-11-04} — folder with full output files from experiment.
  \item \texttt{README.txt} — quick-start instructions and an explicit list of files and their roles.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{A.4 Brief notes}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Prompt sensitivity:} results can shift with different wording; we publish the exact prompt so reviewers can re-run the pipeline with the identical instructions. See \texttt{prompt\_template.txt}.
  \item \textbf{Inherent variation:} in general, even submitting the same prompt twice will not result in identical responses, as can be seen from the results in \texttt{results\_full/raw.jsonl}.
  \item \textbf{Provider selection:} the experiments reported here are illustrative (two exemplar endpoints configured). Other providers, versions, or prompt variants may behave differently.
  \item \textbf{Citation verification:} the analysis pipeline writes raw JSONL so every call can be audited manually; the analysis report documents the spot-checks we performed.
\end{itemize}
