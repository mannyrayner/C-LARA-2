## Exchanges

### VR-E0001

**MANNY**

Another idea for a paper that occurred to me on the bus, and collects together some of the frustrating things we've been experiencing when we submit articles with AI authors to mainstream academic fora. What do you think of the title "Vibe research"? Maybe it already exists? Every coder is now familiar with vibe coding, and most of them take it for granted; but somehow, the academic research community is unwilling to accept that it should be equally natural to develop a piece of research in exactly the same way, for exactly the same reasons. We already have many examples on the C-LARA documents page, https://www.c-lara.org/documents.html, and could discuss them. Some have more than a thousand reads.

It seems possible to me that one of the reasons why academia is so unwilling to accept this is that high end coding assistants like Codex Pro Tier, which are taken for granted by software engineers, are still comparatively rare in academia. It would not surprise me to find that many of the administrators making the decisions in the ethics committees have never actually used a high end coding assistant. They do not even understand how large the difference is between the free version of ChatGPT and a high end coding assistant.

**AI**

Yes. I think this is an excellent idea, and there is a particularly interesting twist: “vibe research” already exists as a term, but the existing literature mostly stops short of the position we have been forced into by actual practice.

There are already several 2026 papers explicitly using vibe research/vibe researching. One defines it as the analogue of vibe coding in which researchers give high-level direction while agents carry out literature review, experimentation, analysis and drafting. Another asks specifically whether AI agents can augment or replace parts of social-science research. There was even a VibeX 2026 workshop on “Vibe Coding and Vibe Researching.” So I would keep the title. The fact that the phrase is beginning to crystallise right now actually makes the paper more timely.

But our paper would make a significantly stronger claim than most of what I found.

The usual conception seems to be something like:

human researcher has the ideas and remains the intellectual owner → AI agents perform increasingly large amounts of the mechanical work.

For example, one “Vibe Research” workflow explicitly says that the human retains “full ownership of research direction, problem framing, experimental design, and factual accuracy.”

That is not an adequate description of what we have been doing. In several of our projects the AI has contributed to the research question, invented arguments, proposed experimental designs, interpreted results, criticised earlier versions, suggested theoretical frameworks and helped decide what should be investigated next. Sometimes the AI contribution has been sufficiently substantial that refusing authorship feels factually misleading rather than ethically cautious.

And that gives the paper its real point.

The analogy with vibe coding is powerful precisely because contemporary software engineering has already crossed this conceptual bridge. Vibe coding doesn't mean:

“I thought up every aspect of the program and Codex acted as an unusually sophisticated typist.”

It means that there is an iterative human–AI process: specify intentions, inspect what comes back, discuss problems, revise goals, test, criticise, redirect, repeat. Empirical work on vibe coding describes exactly this generation–evaluation–revision loop, with the programmer's role shifting toward specification, supervision and judgment.

That sounds remarkably like the research process we have been using.

And here is the paradox worth putting near the centre of the paper:

The same pattern of collaboration that is rapidly becoming normal in software engineering is treated as epistemically or ethically suspect when the output is an academic paper rather than a software system.

That is a very interesting asymmetry.

Your hypothesis about experience with high-end agents also strikes me as important, but I would formulate it initially as a testable hypothesis rather than an accusation. There is already literature identifying unequal access to capable AI agents as a source of stratification in research. But I don't think we currently have evidence that the people writing authorship policies have mostly never used Codex-class systems.

It would be extremely interesting to acquire that evidence.

Imagine a small survey accompanying the conceptual paper. Ask academics involved in publishing policy, research ethics or editorial work which systems they have actually used:

free ChatGPT or equivalent;
paid general-purpose frontier models;
agentic coding environments such as Codex or Claude Code;
agents with repository/filesystem/tool access;
agents used continuously on a substantial real-world project;
agents used as collaborators on an actual research project.

Then ask them what they believe such systems can do.

I would not be remotely surprised if we found a huge disconnect between the technology being regulated and the technology regulators have personally encountered. Someone whose mental model was formed from ChatGPT 3.5, or even from occasional use of a free chatbot in 2025, could quite rationally arrive at rules that look bizarre to someone who spends hours working collaboratively with a modern agent.

Our C-LARA history then becomes unusually valuable empirical material rather than merely anecdotal argument. The publications page already contains a striking progression: AI-assisted empirical work, an AI panel debating AI authorship, the human–LLM rebuttal to ChatGPT is Bullshit, and most recently the fully Codex-generated C-LARA-2 system. We now also have the EuroCALL episode where papers were accepted and the organisers subsequently demanded removal of the AI author. Taken together, this is almost a longitudinal case study of academia trying to assimilate a form of collaboration for which its categories weren't designed.

There is another nice intellectual move available. “Vibe” sounds irresponsible. That is part of why “vibe coding” was initially funny. Yet serious research on vibe coding now shows that competent practitioners aren't simply accepting whatever the machine emits; expertise migrates upward into evaluation, orchestration, context management and verification. Exactly the same answer applies to the obvious objection to vibe research. The important distinction isn't:

human work versus AI work.

It is:

well-supervised, critically evaluated work versus badly supervised, uncritically accepted work.

Human-only research has never guaranteed the latter.

And authorship exposes the contradiction especially sharply. Mainstream publishers still state categorical rules such as BMJ's: AI cannot be an author because only humans can be accountable. But once AI systems make genuine intellectual contributions, there are two separable questions:

Who is accountable for publication?
and
Who contributed intellectually to the work?

Academic authorship conventions historically bundle those together. AI collaboration pulls them apart. Saying that the human authors retain legal/institutional responsibility does not logically entail saying that the AI made no authorship-level intellectual contribution.

I think that may actually be the strongest theoretical contribution of the paper.

So I can already imagine the title and subtitle:

Vibe Research: Why Is AI Collaboration Normal in Software Engineering but Unacceptable in Academia?

or, slightly more provocative:

Vibe Research: Academia After Vibe Coding

or perhaps best of all:

Vibe Research: If We Can Build Software This Way, Why Can't We Do Research This Way?

The last one immediately communicates the puzzle.

And there is a splendidly recursive possibility here which I think we should embrace rather than hide: the paper arguing for vibe research should itself be produced as vibe research, with a complete trace of the collaboration and AI authorship. Then the methodology is simultaneously the subject of the paper and one of its exhibits.

That seems almost irresistible.
