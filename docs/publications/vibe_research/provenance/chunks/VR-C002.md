**MANNY**

Your final version of the title is very nice, clearly the paper should itself be a piece of documented vibe research, and as you say we need to be cautious about making empirical claims which may be hard to justify. A point that occurs to me about "accountability". No one will deny that this is important, but the problem is the lack of clarity about what it actually means. And once again, we can ask: why the asymmetry? In the software case, a vibe coder can develop a piece of software together with a coding assistant. The coding assistant does nearly all the work, including running a large battery of tests which it has itself developed. A sensible human will of course run some more tests themself. But once they've developed some experience with using coding assistants, they'll be able to make reasonable call on how much they need to check, and how much they can trust the assistant, before they ship the code. In practice, formal accountability is with the human (if they screw up too badly, they can get fired), but a lot of the intellectual accountability may rest with the AI. If another team member wants to know the details, the human may well let the AI explain.

**AI**

Yes — and I think this sharpens the paper considerably.

“Accountability” is often invoked as though it were a single, self-explanatory property: AI cannot be accountable, therefore AI cannot be an author. But in actual collaborative work, several quite different things are being bundled together.

There is institutional accountability: who can be disciplined, sued, fired, sanctioned, asked to issue a correction, or required to withdraw the work. That will normally remain with humans or organisations.

There is epistemic accountability: who can explain why a particular decision was made, what evidence supported it, what alternatives were considered, and what would change the conclusion.

There is also something like operational accountability: who actually performed the work, checked it, noticed failures, revised the approach, and decided that the result was adequate.

The software case makes the distinction unusually clear. A human may have institutional responsibility for shipping the system while Codex has done nearly all of the detailed intellectual work: designing the implementation, identifying bugs, creating tests, interpreting their failures and explaining architectural choices. If somebody asks, “Why is this component implemented this way?”, the most competent answer may quite literally come from the AI that implemented it.

And nobody finds this metaphysically puzzling. We don't say, “Codex cannot be fired, therefore it cannot meaningfully explain why it chose this architecture.” We simply distinguish the responsibility of the person who authorised deployment from the provenance of the engineering decisions.

That gives us a very clean question for academia:

Why should publication require us to collapse institutional accountability and intellectual contribution into the same entity when software engineering no longer does?

Indeed, the asymmetry may be even stranger than that. Suppose I use Codex to write a substantial software system, understand its broad architecture, perform enough independent checking to judge it safe to deploy, and accept responsibility for deploying it. This is increasingly regarded as competent professional practice.

Now suppose we collaborate on an empirical paper in exactly the analogous way. I formulate part of the problem; you reformulate it; you propose an experiment; I question one part of it; Codex implements the analysis; you identify an unexpected pattern; I inspect some examples; we revise the annotation scheme; you draft the argument; I challenge a claim; we check the evidence; eventually I decide the paper is sound enough to submit.

Academia can perfectly reasonably say:

“Manny must take responsibility for submitting this.”

What is much harder to justify is the additional inference:

“Therefore ChatGPT made no authorship-level contribution.”

The second proposition simply doesn't follow from the first.

And your point about calibrated trust is important too. The responsible vibe coder does not verify every line of AI-written code personally. If that were necessary, much of the productivity gain would disappear. What expertise increasingly consists in is knowing where verification is necessary, how much evidence is enough, what kinds of mistakes the system tends to make, and when a result is trustworthy.

That is exactly what mature human collaboration already looks like. A principal investigator does not personally rerun every statistical analysis performed by a competent postdoc. An experimentalist may trust a collaborator's spectroscopy without knowing how to operate the instrument. An author may accept another author's account of an archive they themselves cannot read. Accountability in research has always depended on calibrated delegation and distributed expertise.

AI makes the delegation unfamiliar, not conceptually unprecedented.

There is an especially interesting consequence of your “let the AI explain” example. One standard argument against AI authorship is that an author must be able to answer questions about the work. But frontier AI collaborators can now do precisely that — and in some circumstances they can answer detailed questions better than the nominally accountable human author, because they retain or can recover the implementation history, examine the repository, rerun analyses and reconstruct why choices were made.

So one could provocatively turn the conventional argument around:

If authorship entails an obligation to explain one's contribution, the ability of an AI collaborator to explain its contribution is evidence for, rather than against, recognising it.

We would still need to distinguish this from moral or legal accountability. But that's exactly the conceptual cleanup the discussion needs.

I think the paper may therefore end up having two linked asymmetries at its centre:

Software asymmetry: essentially the same human–AI division of labour is accepted as normal professional practice in software engineering and treated as problematic in research.

Accountability asymmetry: academia treats human institutional accountability as though it must entail human intellectual authorship, while software engineering comfortably separates responsibility for shipping something from authorship of its detailed technical content.

That feels much stronger than simply arguing “AIs deserve authorship.” It says that our existing vocabulary has stopped describing contemporary collaborative practice accurately. And Vibe Research gives us a wonderfully concrete way of showing why.
