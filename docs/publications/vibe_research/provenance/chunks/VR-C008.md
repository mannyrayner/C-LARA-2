There is a widespread idea that an AI is not able to do this, but, for reasons already in the current draft, "responsibility" is in practice a slippery concept which is often not helpful,  and that "reliable provenance" (typically entailing ability to explain) will often be more useful.

After all of the preceding theoretical scene-setting (we want at least one forward pointer earlier), we say that the practical question is whether it works: is it in fact possible to set things up so that the AI acts intuitively acts like a responsible research collaborator? Here, I'm thinking that the key reason for believing it to be true is that the formal goals for tracing provenance in a research project are the same as, or very similar to, those for tracing provenance in a software project, so tools which work for one can reasonably be hoped to work for the other. And in fact, high end software assistants do appear to be able to handle research projects in the way they handle software projects. There is one important sociological difference: most researchers are in practice expected to engage with the world research community, but you can easily solve that by wrapping your software assistant in a suitable way so that it's available on the web.

**AI**

Yes. I think this is the right reorganisation, and it gives the paper a much clearer argumentative trajectory. The crucial new idea is that **the comparison with software should do more work than merely motivate the term “vibe research.”** It can give us both the conceptual model and, later, a plausible technical model for how research provenance can actually be implemented.

I’d now organise the paper roughly as follows:

1. **Introduction: From Vibe Coding to Vibe Research.** Introduce Karpathy/vibe coding, but only the aspects we need: delegation, calibrated trust, distributed expertise, the human’s role in deciding what is good enough to ship, and the fact that detailed knowledge may reside in the coding assistant rather than the supervising human. Then pose the central asymmetry: what happens if essentially the same working method is applied to research? Give an explicit forward pointer that later we will ask not only whether the analogy is conceptually defensible, but whether current software-agent technology can actually support the provenance and explanatory requirements of research.

2. **What Is Vibe Research? Similarities and Differences.** Define it by analogy with vibe coding, then immediately discuss where the analogy might break. I think your sociological point should be central here. Research is not merely production of research artefacts. To be a researcher conventionally involves participation in a research community, and authorship is one of the mechanisms through which that participation is organised. Authors don't merely produce a paper; they're supposed, in some sense, to stand behind it and engage with other researchers about it.

   This is where we introduce the apparent obstacle: *an AI cannot be a responsible member of the research community*. But rather than trying to refute that immediately, we say that the meaning of “responsible” turns out to be surprisingly unclear. What researchers often need in practice is something more concrete: **reliable provenance plus the ability to explain the relevant intellectual decisions**. That tees up the next two sections beautifully.

3. **Responsibility, Expertise, and Distributed Accountability.** Combine the current senior–junior section and institutional/intellectual accountability section. The PI, junior researcher, senior engineer and coding-assistant examples all belong here. The main analytical move is: institutional responsibility for deciding to publish or ship does not require the responsible person to contain all of the relevant expertise. We already accept distributed intellectual accountability in human organisations. The human may be answerable for the *decision*, while another team member is answerable for the *details*.

4. **Provenance, Authorship, and Ephemeral Expertise.** Combine the current provenance/authorship and disappearing-expertise material. I think this section becomes stronger once “provenance” has been established as the practical desideratum. The question is no longer “does an AI deserve authorship?” but “how should the scholarly record represent substantial intellectual provenance?” The loss-of-access example is then not an appendix to the argument; it shows exactly why provenance matters. Expertise can leave a project when a human leaves, or when an AI context/model/account becomes unavailable. Naming and preserving provenance is one way scholarly and technical communities protect against this.

Then I think the paper should make a conspicuous transition, something like **“So far, the argument has been conceptual. The obvious practical question is whether the proposed arrangement actually works.”**

5. **Can an AI Act as a Research Collaborator?** This is the new centre of gravity, and I think it's much more interesting than the corresponding section in our current draft.

   We don't need to claim that an AI is “responsible” in some philosophically loaded sense. We can operationalise the question. Can it preserve and retrieve the provenance of decisions? Can it explain why analyses were performed? Can it inspect the relevant artefacts? Can it distinguish what was found from what was conjectured? Can it answer questions from other researchers about its contribution?

   And then comes what I think is your strongest technical argument: **these requirements overlap heavily with the requirements already encountered in serious software projects.** A capable coding agent needs repository access, project history, documentation, tests, issue context, and the ability to inspect artefacts and explain them. A research project adds papers, experimental data, analysis scripts, research discussions and perhaps literature, but structurally this is strikingly similar.

   So there is an empirical reason for optimism. We don't have to hypothesise an entirely new “AI researcher” architecture. High-end coding assistants are already very close to the required architecture.

6. **From Private Assistant to Public Research Collaborator.** I think this deserves its own short section because it answers the strongest remaining sociological objection.

   A normal coding agent is private because that's what software projects usually want. But privacy is not intrinsic to the architecture. Give the agent controlled access to the research repository and expose a suitable interface on the web, and other researchers can interrogate it.

   The difference between “Codex working privately with Manny” and “an AI research collaborator that other researchers can question” can therefore be, technologically, **a thin web wrapper**.

   That's a lovely result because it makes the sociology/technology distinction very explicit. The apparent inability of an AI collaborator to participate in the research community may sometimes be less a limitation of the AI than a consequence of how we conventionally deploy it.

7. **Three Examples of Vibe Research and Publicly Accessible Provenance.** Here we finally cash the cheque.

   C-LARA-2 is the big case: large, sustained, software-heavy, months of accumulated context and serious technical complexity. The public Assistant already lets people ask about the platform's functioning; the documents page explicitly describes C-LARA-2 as a complete rewrite in which “100% of the work” was performed by Codex and highlights its ability to answer detailed questions about itself. ([C-LARA][1])

   The Voice Mode paper gives us a second case in the same infrastructure but with a rather different type of research: an informal longitudinal empirical study involving eight 60–90 minute language-learning sessions and subsequent analysis. ([C-LARA][1])

   And I **strongly favour adding *How Woke is Grok?*** as the third. It gives us something the other two cannot: a tiny, opportunistic research project that exploited very low research transaction costs. The PsyPost account actually quotes you explaining exactly this genesis: you saw something puzzling about Grokipedia and realised there was “a simple experiment we could quickly carry out” by adapting the previous experiment. ([PsyPost - Psychology News][2]) That is almost a textbook example of one potential benefit of vibe research.

And the PsyPost coverage makes it especially useful because it demonstrates that **quick and inexpensive does not imply trivial or socially invisible**. PsyPost published a six-minute article devoted to the study on November 14, 2025, describing the experiment and its findings in considerable detail. ([PsyPost - Psychology News][2]) ResearchGate also records it as a November 2025 preprint coauthored by you and ChatGPT C-LARA-Instance, with all code, prompts and analysis artefacts made public. ([ResearchGate][3]) I couldn't independently verify the current 1,300-read figure from the publicly indexed page, so I'd treat that as a number to snapshot from ResearchGate when we prepare the paper rather than relying on it until then.

I also think copying *How Woke is Grok?* into the C-LARA-2 repo is more than presentation polish. **It creates an experimental demonstration of the paper's thesis.** We can expose three substantially different pieces of research through essentially the same mechanism and invite readers to interrogate the AI collaborator about each one.

That leads to a very attractive empirical moment in the paper. Instead of saying:

> We believe an appropriately configured AI could satisfy many of the practical requirements associated with intellectual accountability.

we can effectively say:

> Here are three research projects. Here is the AI collaborator. Ask it.

That is vastly more persuasive.

After that, I would put the more speculative sociology:

8. **Why the Asymmetry?** Now we have earned the right to ask why research reacts differently. Differences in professional credit, the permanent scholarly record, institutional traditions, familiarity with high-end agents, etc. We can discuss possibilities cautiously rather than trying to prove any of them.

9. **How Common Is Vibe Research?** Here go the disclosure-incentives argument and the proposed expression-of-interest mechanism. The conjecture is that we are unusual principally in *saying explicitly what we are doing*, not in doing it. But we label that clearly as a conjecture needing data.

10. **This Paper as Vibe Research / Discussion / Conclusion.** These can probably be fairly short. Indeed, “This Paper...” might ultimately work better as a subsection of the empirical section or Discussion rather than another full section.

There is one phrase in your formulation that I'd handle carefully: **“acts intuitively like a responsible research collaborator.”** I agree completely with what you mean, but I think the paper gains from refusing to define *responsible* at precisely the point where everyone else assumes they know what it means. I'd say something closer to:

> Can an AI collaborator fulfil the practical functions for which responsibility is invoked in discussions of research collaboration?

Then enumerate those functions. Some — legal liability, employment sanctions — plainly no. Others — explaining decisions, recovering provenance, defending an analysis, inspecting evidence, responding to criticism — quite possibly yes.

And that, I think, gives us a much stronger overall architecture:

**vibe coding → vibe research → the sociological difference → what “responsibility” actually requires → provenance as the tractable requirement → software provenance as an existence proof → public wrapping of the agent → three working demonstrations → why academia treats this differently → how widespread the practice actually is.**

That has a real argumentative arc. The previous version was essentially a collection of good observations about the same topic; this version feels like a paper.
