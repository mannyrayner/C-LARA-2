**AI**

Yes. I agree with all seven, and I think they point toward a noticeably tighter paper.

On 1–2, I would make the authorship issue more explicit in the Introduction and then avoid substantially reintroducing it in §2. Something close to your wording is better because it describes the actual social fact without prematurely endorsing or attacking it:

> This is often phrased as some version of the question “Can the AI be a responsible author?”, with the implication that it cannot. In current academic practice, AI systems are generally excluded from authorship.

And we can back that up very cleanly. ICMJE says AI tools “should not be listed as authors” because they cannot be responsible for accuracy, integrity and originality. Nature-family policies use almost exactly the accountability argument: LLMs do not satisfy authorship criteria because authorship carries accountability which “cannot be effectively applied to LLMs.” ([ICMJE][1]) Even the current Springer Nature framework explicitly says that human accountability is non-transferable and categorises “assigning authorship or accountability to AI systems” as impermissible. ([Nature][2])

That gives us something quite strong to analyse because **“accountability” isn't our straw man; it is literally the terminology used in the policies.**

I also agree completely on 3. The current public-interface section is overdeveloped relative to the simplicity of the point. §5 can end by saying, in effect: coding agents are normally private because their repositories are private; for public research artefacts, a thin web wrapper can expose the same project-grounded agent to the research community. Then straight into the examples.

On 4, yes. At present those examples prove the point but don't give a reader enough sense of what the research actually was. I think each one needs three things: **the research question, the human–AI workflow, and what the public interface can now be asked about.** The contrast among the three is then genuinely informative rather than decorative:

* C-LARA-2: a long-running, technically large reconstruction project that itself generates research questions about AI-developed software.
* Voice Mode: an empirical/qualitative study based on sustained real-world interaction, very different from software construction.
* *How Woke is Grok?*: a short opportunistic empirical study, apparently about two days' work, where drastically lower research overhead made it rational to test a question that might otherwise have remained a hunch.

That third example is particularly good because it demonstrates another possible meaning of “vibe research”: not merely doing conventional projects faster, but **changing the set of questions that are worth investigating**.

On 5, agreed. “What the examples establish” and “Why the asymmetry?” can become one section, perhaps **“What Do These Examples Tell Us?”** It can first delimit the evidence and then return to the asymmetry. That avoids the slightly school-essay rhythm of demonstration followed by separate caveat followed by separate interpretation.

Your point 6 is the one where I think the new evidence really improves the paper. We no longer need to be excessively coy and say merely “we suspect” substantial AI use is widespread. There is now strong population-level evidence.

Liang et al. analysed nearly **951,000 papers** across arXiv, bioRxiv and Nature-family journals and estimated LLM modification reaching **17.5% in computer science** in their studied period; importantly, their method is explicitly corpus-level and not intended to prove that a particular individual paper was AI-produced. ([arXiv][3]) Kobak et al. analysed **more than 15 million PubMed abstracts** and estimated that at least **13.5% of 2024 biomedical abstracts** had been processed using LLMs, with much higher rates in some subcorpora. ([arXiv][4]) Gray's later cross-disciplinary analysis estimated likely LLM involvement in **more than 10% of all published papers in 2024** and specifically discusses evidence that such use is often undisclosed or downplayed. ([arXiv][5])

So we can say something considerably firmer:

**Large-scale statistical studies leave little doubt that LLM involvement in academic writing is already widespread. What they generally cannot determine is the intellectual depth of that involvement in individual papers.**

And that distinction is perfect for us. These studies establish widespread AI involvement in the *observable textual record*. They don't tell us how many researchers are doing what we call vibe research — where the AI participates substantively in the research itself. **That** becomes our unanswered empirical question.

I would be more cautious about the CFP-submission claim. I agree that there is widespread anecdotal perception of sharply increased submission volumes and obvious low-effort AI papers, but unless we find decent quantitative or documented conference evidence, we should label that explicitly as anecdotal. We don't need it for the argument now that the large-scale publication studies are so strong.

Finally, yes on 7. “This Paper as Vibe Research” can be folded into a shortened Discussion/Conclusion. In fact I think the recursion is stronger if mentioned briefly in the Introduction — “the present paper is itself produced and documented in this way” — and then returned to at the end. It doesn't need a whole section.

So I now see the structure as something like:

1. **Introduction: From Vibe Coding to Vibe Research**
2. **What Is Vibe Research?**
3. **Responsibility, Expertise, and Distributed Accountability**
4. **Provenance, Authorship, and Ephemeral Expertise**
5. **Can Software-Agent Infrastructure Support Research Provenance?**
6. **Three Documented Examples**

   * C-LARA-2
   * Voice Mode
   * *How Woke is Grok?*
7. **What Do These Examples Tell Us?**
8. **How Widespread Is Vibe Research?**
9. **Discussion and Conclusion**

That's substantially leaner: nine sections, with a much clearer break between **conceptual argument**, **technical/practical demonstration**, and **empirical prevalence**.

And one other thought: §8 could make a particularly nice distinction between three increasingly strong claims:

**AI-assisted writing is widespread** — already strongly evidenced.
**substantive AI participation in research is widespread** — plausible, but not yet well measured.
**vibe research as we define it is widespread** — our empirical conjecture.

That would exemplify exactly the evidential discipline we want the whole paper to project.
