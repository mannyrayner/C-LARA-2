[Casey Newton article]

**AI**

Yes — surprisingly relevant, and in several different ways. Cathy has found something that fits the paper almost embarrassingly well.

The strongest point is probably Shipper’s “dirty secret” claim:

> “almost every writer is using it … most of them are not saying so.”

That is anecdotal rather than population-level evidence, so we shouldn’t use it to establish prevalence. But it is excellent evidence that **the disclosure problem we are discussing is not peculiar to academia**: there can be a large gap between actual AI-mediated practice and publicly acknowledged practice when prevailing social norms make disclosure uncomfortable.

There are at least four other places where this is unusually useful.

First, **the software/research asymmetry**. Every is almost a natural experiment because the *same organisation* occupies both worlds. Shipper says AI writes essentially all of Every’s code, while humans still mostly write the essays.  That gives us a striking miniature version of our starting puzzle: extensive AI delegation has become normal on one side of the organisation while authorship and AI contribution remain socially fraught on the writing side.

Second, his description of the engineering transformation is very close to our account of calibrated supervision. He says one person can now run an entire software product end-to-end, and later sums up the change as engineering having “moved up a level.”   That gives us independent contemporary testimony for the practical premise behind our analogy: the skilled human role does not disappear when detailed implementation is delegated; it changes.

Third — and I think this may be even more interesting — **the Kate Lee example is almost a case study in distributed and preservable expertise**. Every collected 30,000 historical edits from its editor-in-chief, used them to construct and back-test an editing agent, and now uses that agent to distribute aspects of her expertise throughout the company. Shipper explicitly describes the goal not as eliminating the expert but as capturing some of her “taste and viewpoint and set of skills” in a reusable tool.

That is extremely close to our provenance argument, though viewed from the opposite direction. We have been saying that expertise can be distributed between a human and an AI and that a good infrastructure should preserve access to it. Here is a real organisation deliberately **externalising part of a human expert’s intellectual contribution into an AI-accessible form** because relying exclusively on the human expert does not scale. It makes the boundary between “human expertise” and “AI expertise” look much less clean than standard authorship rhetoric tends to assume.

And fourth, the exchange about authorship is directly germane. Shipper argues that whether the writer physically typed the words is not the decisive issue, comparing AI-generated changes with accepting changes from a human editor. Newton immediately pushes on precisely the sociological point we have been discussing: people have much stronger reactions if an AI drafts a whole passage. Shipper’s answer is that the norms are still “in pencil” and that a mainstream audience is much more sensitive than an audience accustomed to AI as an everyday technology.

That gives us a source for a point we have so far been treating cautiously as our own hypothesis: **familiarity with the technology may strongly influence intuitions about acceptable collaboration.** It still doesn’t prove our more specific conjecture about academic administrators and high-end coding assistants, of course. But it shows that someone running an organisation built around extensive AI use explicitly perceives this familiarity gap between communities.

I think I would use the Newton/Shipper piece in three places rather than create a new discussion around it:

* In the **Introduction or §2**, a sentence noting that even within a single AI-intensive organisation, extensive delegation in software is treated differently from AI contribution to signed writing.
* In **§3–4**, the Kate Lee example as a particularly clean illustration of expertise being externalised, distributed and made recoverable.
* In **“How Widespread Is Vibe Research?”**, immediately after the large statistical studies: those establish widespread LLM involvement in scholarly writing; Shipper independently reports a social phenomenon in professional writing in which substantial AI use may be common while public acknowledgement lags behind practice. We should label his statement explicitly as an informed anecdotal observation, not statistical evidence.

There is also a lovely sentence buried in the interview which could almost be an epigraph for our whole paper. Shipper says that for people accustomed to the technology, it “feels like a part of everyday life, as opposed to this new big threatening thing.”

That is pretty much the sociological asymmetry we have been circling.

And yes, I would definitely add the Newton piece to `references.bib`. More importantly, I think it slightly changes my view of the paper: **we now have an external case showing that the same conceptual tensions arise at the boundary between vibe coding and another prestige-producing, individually attributed intellectual activity — professional writing.** That makes our claim look less like an eccentricity of academic publishing and more like one instance of a broader transition in how communities understand intellectual contribution.
