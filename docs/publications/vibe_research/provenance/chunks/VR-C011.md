### VR-E0047

**MANNY**

Again, we agree here.

Another point that occurred to me, which may be relevant to explaining the different views of vibe coding in the developer community and "vibe research" in the research community. People in the developer community are now generally assumed to be familiar with agentic use of AI, coding assistants just being the most obvious manifestation of this. But my impression is that this is not true in the research community, at least not for the people who are making policy. E.g. looking at this section https://www.aclweb.org/adminwiki/index.php/ACL_Policy_on_Publication_Ethics#Guidelines_for_Generative_Assistance_in_Authorship of the "ACL Policy on Publication Ethics" document, the immediate impression is that it is based on pre-agentic paradigm which has not reflected current practice for some time. What do you think?

**AI**

I think this is potentially an important part of the explanation, but it needs exactly the evidential caution we’ve been aiming for.

Looking at the current ACL policy, your “pre-agentic paradigm” impression is quite understandable. Its definition of generative tools is framed around systems that “generate multimedia outputs,” with examples centred on text/image generation and privacy. ([ACL Member Portal][1]) More tellingly, the permitted-use taxonomy is organised around things like language polishing, typing assistance, literature search, low-novelty text, “new ideas,” and “new ideas + new text.” ([ACL Member Portal][1])

That is basically a **prompt-in, content-out model** of AI assistance. It does not describe the workflow we have been calling vibe research: an agent with persistent access to a project, files, code, tools, experiment outputs and project history, repeatedly acting, inspecting results, debugging, revising and recovering earlier decisions.

The “new ideas + new text” category is especially revealing. ACL simply says that it does not consider a generative model capable of satisfying co-authorship requirements. ([ACL Member Portal][1]) But the policy’s preceding categories don’t really contain a conceptual slot for something like:

> the AI proposed the experiment, wrote and ran the analysis code, inspected unexpected outputs, modified the experiment, discussed the interpretation with the human, drafted part of the argument, and can subsequently reopen the complete project and explain what happened.

That isn’t just “new ideas + new text.” It’s a qualitatively different interaction model.

There’s actually a rather striking internal tension in the ACL policy which could be useful to us. Its general authorship criteria say that someone who makes a substantial contribution to conception, design, acquisition, analysis or interpretation must then be given the opportunity to participate in drafting/revision and final approval; the policy explicitly says people should not be denied authorship by preventing them from satisfying those later criteria. ([ACL Member Portal][1]) Then, when the contributor is an AI, the generative-assistance section categorically says it cannot fulfil co-authorship requirements. ([ACL Member Portal][1])

We shouldn’t present that as a contradiction, because ACL’s definition of “Author” is explicitly “any person” named in the Authors field, and their accountability criterion is framed in human institutional terms. ([ACL Member Portal][1]) But it makes our point very cleanly: **the framework has decided in advance that the familiar human pathway from substantial intellectual contribution to authorship terminates when the contributor is an AI.** The reason offered is responsibility/accountability, which brings us straight back to our distinction.

I also think your “skipping a generation” formulation is useful conceptually. I’d distinguish roughly:

**chatbot paradigm** → give a prompt, receive some text;

**tool-using assistant paradigm** → model can search, calculate, run code, inspect files, etc., but interaction is still substantially task-by-task;

**agentic project paradigm** → persistent structured context, autonomous multi-step execution, inspection of its own outputs, use of tools and repositories, iterative correction, and the ability to return later to the evolving state of a substantial project.

The difference between the first and third is enormous. If somebody thinks of “using ChatGPT in research” mainly as asking a chatbot to write paragraphs, then many existing policies make intuitive sense. If their reference case is six months of working with a repository-scale agent that implements experiments, runs tests, updates project records and can later explain what it did, the same vocabulary starts to feel badly underspecified.

The really attractive hypothesis for the paper is therefore not “academic policymakers don’t understand AI.” That would be both inflammatory and impossible for us currently to substantiate. I’d phrase it more like:

> One possible contributor to the asymmetry is a difference in the AI interaction paradigms familiar to the two communities. Agentic workflows have become increasingly routine in software engineering, while publication policies still often conceptualise generative AI primarily in terms of discrete generation of text or other content.

That first sentence is a hypothesis. The second we can support by analysing policies such as ACL’s.

Then we can say that our informal experience motivates a further empirical question: **how familiar are researchers, editors and research-policy makers with sustained agentic workflows?** In particular, do attitudes to AI participation differ according to whether someone has actually used a high-end project-scale agent?

That could become a very nice component of the eventual survey. Not “have you used ChatGPT?”, which would tell us almost nothing, but something like a staged capability inventory:

* Have you used a conversational LLM?
* Have you used an LLM with tools/code execution?
* Have you used an agent that can independently perform multi-step tasks?
* Have you used an agent with persistent access to a substantial project/repository?
* Have you worked with such an agent repeatedly over weeks or months on the same project?

Then we could correlate those answers with views on AI contribution, accountability and authorship.

There is another reason the ACL example is especially valuable: **ACL is about as technically AI-literate an academic community as one could choose.** If even its publication policy is most naturally expressed in a generation-centric taxonomy, we don’t need to speculate about some technologically naive ethics committee elsewhere. The stronger and safer observation is that **institutional categories are lagging behind the fastest-moving working practices even inside the community closest to the technology.**

That, I think, belongs in “What Do the Examples Tell Us?” or perhaps a short subsection in the asymmetry discussion. It gives us a concrete candidate explanation for the asymmetry without overclaiming anything about the motives or competence of the people writing the policies.

**AI**

Yes — the revision history makes this considerably more interesting, and it supports a strong claim, though not quite the strongest version of it.

The ACL policy was created in June 2024, and the original June 18 version already contains essentially the present six-part taxonomy: language assistance, short-form input, literature search, low-novelty text, new ideas, and “new ideas + new text,” followed by the categorical statement that ACL does not consider a generative model capable of co-authorship. ([ACL Member Portal][1])

What is striking is what happened afterwards. The policy as a whole was certainly maintained: there were substantive revisions in April/May 2025, a large new Scientific Integrity section in July 2025, definition changes in September 2025, and further edits in 2026. ([ACL Member Portal][2]) Yet the **Generative Assistance in Authorship section remained essentially word-for-word unchanged at least through July 20, 2025**. Compare the original June 2024 text with the July 2025 version: the same categories and the same categorical co-authorship sentence remain. ([ACL Member Portal][1])

The present version has acquired a few additions — for example, explicit references to rebuttals and the statement that uses not listed are inappropriate — but its conceptual taxonomy remains the same: it is still organised around what a generative model outputs rather than around an agent participating over time in a project. ([ACL Member Portal][3])

That seems to give us a genuinely solid observation:

> **The ACL publication-ethics framework has been actively maintained since 2024, but its model of generative assistance continues to be organised around a generation-centric taxonomy introduced before project-scale agentic workflows became widespread.**

That is much stronger than merely saying “the page looks old.” And it avoids claiming anything about *why* this happened.

I think “policy inertia” then becomes a reasonable **hypothesis suggested by the evidence**, but not something we can assert as proven. The interesting thing is that this isn't simply an abandoned policy document. ACL repeatedly revised other parts of it while leaving this conceptual treatment substantially intact. That makes the lag worth asking about.

There is another rather nice historical datum. ACL’s current page explicitly says it was approved on June 15, 2024 and re-approved on April 25, July 19 and September 26, 2025. ([ACL Member Portal][3]) So one could even say:

> The persistence of the framework cannot straightforwardly be attributed to the policy simply having escaped institutional attention: the document was repeatedly reconsidered and approved while the generative-authorship taxonomy remained substantially unchanged.

Again, we mustn't infer that someone specifically reconsidered the AI section each time. “The document was re-approved” is the fact; “they considered agentic AI and rejected it” would not be justified.

### The appeal point needs one qualification

I checked this because I thought your statement about lack of appeal might be exactly right. ACL **does have a formal appeals procedure**, so we should not say simply that there is “no well-defined appeals process.” A complainant or respondent dissatisfied with the outcome of a publication-ethics case may appeal to the ACL Executive Committee, which can order a new investigation. ([ACL Member Portal][4])

But that procedure appears designed for **adjudicating complaints and sanctions**, not for a researcher who says, “I think this standing policy is conceptually obsolete; whom do I ask to reconsider it?” The process document gives a formal complaint route and says that uncovered issues may lead the PEC to develop policy amendments, but I don't see a comparably explicit procedure through which an ordinary author can request review of an existing policy position. ([ACL Member Portal][4])

So there is a subtler and more interesting point available:

> ACL provides a detailed process for adjudicating alleged violations of publication-ethics policy, including an appeal mechanism, but it is much less clear what formal mechanism is available to an author wishing to challenge or seek reconsideration of the policy itself.

That is factually defensible and, frankly, more revealing than saying there is no appeal system.

### Your experiences matter, but differently

I wouldn't discard the ISCA, ACL and journal episodes. Collectively they're actually very useful. But I would treat them as **documented participant experience**, not external empirical evidence.

The ISCA sequence you describe is especially revealing because it seems to run through three quite different rationales:

legal responsibility → what if the humans die? → people aren't ready for AI co-authors.

If we have a contemporaneous written record of that exchange, it could potentially be reported quite neutrally. Something like:

> In one exchange with a member of a professional-society ethics committee, we asked why an AI collaborator could not be listed as an author. The initial explanation concerned legal responsibility. When we noted that human co-authors could retain formal responsibility, discussion moved first to the hypothetical unavailability of the human authors and finally to the observation that the community was not yet ready to accept AI co-authors.

No rhetoric is necessary. Readers can see for themselves that these are three substantially different arguments.

If it was only an oral conversation, I'd be more cautious still: “In our recollection of an informal discussion...” perhaps. The underlying point then isn't that the committee behaved badly. It is that **the rationale for the policy appears difficult to articulate consistently when tested against concrete counterexamples.**

Likewise, I would not write that the ACL committee is “reluctant to discuss the issues” based on one unanswered email after a week and a half. That's exactly the sort of sentence an unsympathetic reviewer can attack. We can say something narrower if it remains true by submission time:

> Our attempts to obtain clarification of the rationale behind the policy have so far not produced a substantive response.

Dates, recipients/roles, and what was asked can be documented if necessary. No inference about motives.

The journal incident is potentially stronger evidence about **incentives against disclosure**, particularly if the correspondence explicitly says that the paper was being rejected because of your position on AI authorship. If we possess those emails, that's documentary evidence of exactly the phenomenon we discuss: openly describing an AI collaborator can place publication itself at risk. But I would probably anonymise the journal unless there is a compelling reason not to. The paper isn't an exposé.

### I think this gives us a new, quite powerful piece of §7

We could now distinguish **technological change from institutional update rate**.

Software practice can change extremely quickly because an individual developer can simply start using a better agent tomorrow. If it works, colleagues copy the practice. There doesn't need to be a committee meeting to redefine what programming is.

Academic publication practices are different. They are encoded in policies, authorship definitions, submission systems, ethics committees and publisher rules. Changing them requires institutional action. So even if individual researchers rapidly adopt agentic workflows, **the vocabulary used to regulate them can remain anchored to an earlier technological paradigm.**

ACL gives us a remarkably good case because nobody can plausibly dismiss ACL as a community ignorant of AI. The observation is instead institutional:

**a technologically sophisticated community can have policy machinery whose conceptual model updates substantially more slowly than the technology and working practices of its members.**

That is a much more interesting claim than “ethics committees don't understand AI.”

And it fits Casey Newton/Shipper beautifully. The Every interview gives us the fast-moving side: norms are “in pencil,” actual practice changes as model capability changes, and people familiar with the technology develop different intuitions.  The ACL history gives us the slow-moving side: a formal taxonomy created in June 2024 survives substantially intact through repeated institutional revisions.

I think **that contrast may be one of our best candidate explanations for the asymmetry**:

**software engineering updates primarily through practice; academic publishing updates partly through governance. Practice can change on the timescale of model releases, while governance generally cannot.**

We needn't say that governance *should* move as fast — there are good reasons for caution. But when the underlying technology jumps from chatbot → tool-using assistant → persistent project agent in a very short period, ordinary institutional inertia can leave policy regulating a workflow that is no longer the one some researchers are actually using.

That feels both strong and fair.

I think this version has one major structural improvement over the previous one: the paper now has two answers to the asymmetry, at different levels.

The first is about what the work actually requires: research has social requirements around authorship and accountability, but those become more tractable when we separate institutional accountability from provenance.

The second is about how norms change: developers can adopt a new praxis directly, while academics must interact with a governance layer.
