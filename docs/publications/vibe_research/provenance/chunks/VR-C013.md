The ACL passage

Here I think your concern is exactly right. On rereading the exact policy, the meaning of item (f) is genuinely under-specified.

The relevant sequence is:

item (e), “New ideas,” explicitly covers cases where model output appears to contain ideas which, if supplied by a human, “would deserve co-authorship or acknowledgment”; the human authors then develop those ideas themselves;
item (f) is simply “New ideas + new text: ACL does not consider a generative model to be an entity that can fulfill the requirements of co-authorship.”

And immediately before the taxonomy ACL says both that generative AI tools may not be listed as authors and that any use not listed below is considered inappropriate.

So I agree that at least three readings are available:

Capability reading: “new ideas + new text” would normally look like authorship-level contribution, but generative models cannot in fact meet the other authorship criteria.
Status reading: even if the model makes contributions that would otherwise look authorship-level, ACL does not regard the model as an entity eligible for co-authorship.
Permissibility reading: because the model cannot be a co-author, allowing it to contribute both new ideas and new text may itself be outside the permitted use cases.

The wording does not clearly tell the reader which of these is intended.

In fact, the surrounding text makes the ambiguity more striking. ACL’s general authorship criteria say that authorship requires substantial contribution, critical drafting/revision, final approval and accountability, and add that someone who contributes to experiments must be given the opportunity to participate in the remaining criteria. Yet the generative-AI section then declares AI authorship unavailable without spelling out which of these requirements is regarded as intrinsically impossible for an agentic system.

I think that gives us a better argument than our previous one.

We should not say that ACL straightforwardly classifies agentic work as “new ideas + new text.” Instead, say that the taxonomy becomes difficult to apply once AI participation exceeds the generation-centric cases it was apparently designed around.

Something like this:

The interpretation of the final category is itself unclear. It may mean that a generative model cannot in fact satisfy the remaining requirements of co-authorship; that even if it makes otherwise authorship-level contributions it is categorically ineligible for authorship; or that allowing it to contribute both new ideas and new text is therefore impermissible. The policy does not disambiguate these possibilities. This ambiguity becomes increasingly consequential for project-scale agentic workflows, where the AI may contribute not only ideas and prose but also experimental design, implementation, execution, diagnosis, revision, and subsequent explanation.

Then instead of the problematic sentence:

“Calling this ‘new ideas + new text’ is not exactly false...”

I’d use:

“Such a workflow does not fit cleanly into the taxonomy. Describing it merely in terms of generated ideas and text omits much of the relevant activity, while the policy does not specify how a sustained agentic collaborator making these additional contributions should be classified.”

That is both stronger and safer. We are no longer telling ACL what its policy means. We are pointing out, demonstrably, that a researcher trying to apply the policy to an agentic workflow cannot easily determine what it means.

And that actually reinforces the governance-lag argument. A policy does not need to contain an obviously wrong sentence in order to lag behind practice. It is enough that new forms of work no longer map cleanly onto the categories through which the policy regulates them.

I think this is a much better formulation for a paper that hostile readers may examine sentence by sentence.
