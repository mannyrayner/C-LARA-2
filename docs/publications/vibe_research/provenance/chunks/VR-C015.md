### VR-E0056

**MANNY**

I have been working with Codex. Everything should now be in the repo, and we have made progress in other directions, in particular citing a recent paper by David Gunkel which looks relevant.

I just had an idea for an intro which might at least amuse Australians. Are you familiar with the tagline "It's the vibe" from the Australian classic movie "The Castle"?

**AI**

Very familiar. In *The Castle*, Dennis Denuto’s gloriously inadequate constitutional argument culminates in “It’s the vibe,” after fumbling around with “Mabo” and the Constitution. It has become one of those Australian phrases that can mean roughly: *the underlying principle is obvious even if I can’t quite formulate the legal doctrine*.

And yes, I think there may be a genuinely good opening for this paper hiding in it.

Something like:

> In the Australian film *The Castle*, an inexperienced lawyer attempting to formulate a constitutional argument eventually falls back on the memorable phrase: “It’s the vibe.” The line became an Australian cultural shorthand for an intuition which seems compelling even when its formal justification remains elusive.
>
> The term *vibe coding* began in something of the same spirit. But the joke has rapidly turned into a serious software-development methodology. This paper asks what happens when essentially the same methodology is applied to research.

Then we pivot immediately to Karpathy and the actual definition.

The nice thing is that it does several jobs at once. It makes the title memorable, acknowledges the slightly comic connotations of “vibe,” and sets up one of the paper’s deeper themes: **practice can become well established before the conceptual and institutional vocabulary has caught up with it.**

I wouldn’t overdo the joke, though. Two or three sentences and then straight into the serious argument. Australians will smile; everyone else can understand it from the explanation.

And there is an extra little resonance which I rather like: Denuto’s problem is that he senses there is a valid principle but cannot express it using the established legal categories. That is not entirely unrelated to our claim that current research-governance categories have trouble describing what is actually happening in agentic research workflows.

### VR-E0057

**MANNY**

Exactly. Dennis Denuto knows he's right but can't say why; though when he gets help from an experienced QC who can explain it in formal legal language, they win the case. Here, we are trying to be the QC and the new phrase "vibe research" respectable.

**AI**

Exactly. That makes the analogy much better than a mere cultural joke.

Denuto has the right intuition but not the language needed to make it institutionally legible. The QC’s contribution is not to invent a different case; it is to **translate the intuition into the conceptual vocabulary the court can recognise**. That is very close to what we are trying to do here.

The practical reality already exists: people are working with AI systems in sustained, iterative, intellectually substantive ways. “Vibe research” gives that practice a memorable name, but the paper’s real job is to supply the more formal account underneath it: distributed expertise, institutional versus intellectual accountability, reliable provenance, agentic project infrastructure, and the governance lag between research practice and publication policy.

So I think the opening could quite explicitly say that. Something along these lines:

> In the Australian film *The Castle*, the inexperienced lawyer Dennis Denuto is convinced that his clients have a valid constitutional case, but struggles to express the underlying principle in formal legal terms. His argument eventually collapses into the memorable phrase, “It’s the vibe.” Only when an experienced barrister reformulates the intuition in the language recognised by the court does the case succeed.
>
> The expression *vibe coding* began with something of the same deliberately informal quality. It names a practice whose effectiveness was first established pragmatically, before a settled conceptual vocabulary had developed around it. In this paper, we ask whether an analogous practice exists in academic research. Our aim is, in part, to do for “vibe research” what the senior barrister does for Denuto’s intuition: to identify more precisely what the practice consists of, why it works when it works, and how it relates to established concepts such as authorship, accountability and intellectual provenance.

I think that is charming without being frivolous. More importantly, it states our methodological stance rather nicely: **we are not asking people to accept “the vibe” as an argument; we are trying to unpack the vibe into an argument.**

And there is perhaps an even better payoff at the end of the introduction: after all the formal setup, we could say something very restrained like, “The vibe, in other words, requires an account.” That may be one Australianism too many, but I rather like it.
