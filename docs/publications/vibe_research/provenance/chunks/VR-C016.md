### 4. I think this substantially improves *The Castle* opening

The key isn't merely that “vibe” occurs in both. It's the **structure of the joke**.

At first we're invited to laugh at Denuto because “it's the vibe” plainly isn't adequate legal reasoning. Then the film reverses the joke: Denuto's intuition was substantially right; what he lacked was the conceptual machinery to express it in institutionally acceptable terms.

Vibe coding has undergone rather a similar reversal. “Forget that the code even exists” sounded deliberately absurd in Karpathy's original formulation. But the technology improved so fast that something close to the joke became normal professional practice.

That lets us pose a deliciously restrained question about vibe research. We don't need to say academia is ridiculous for laughing. We can say: **perhaps we should hesitate before assuming that the joke settles the issue, because we have just watched essentially the same joke undergo an extremely rapid reclassification in software engineering.**

I'd rewrite the opening roughly this way:

> In the Australian film \emph{The Castle}, the inexperienced lawyer Dennis Denuto is convinced that his clients have a valid constitutional case, but is unable to express the underlying principle in formal legal terms. His argument eventually collapses into the memorable phrase, ``It's the vibe.'' At first, the audience is invited to laugh: whatever else legal reasoning may consist of, this surely cannot be enough. But the joke is ultimately on us. Denuto's intuition is substantially correct. When an experienced barrister reformulates it in language recognised by the court, the case succeeds.
>
> The expression \emph{vibe coding} began with something of the same comic quality. Andrej Karpathy's original description deliberately sounded like the opposite of serious software engineering: delegate the coding to an AI, ``forget that the code even exists'', and work by describing what you want and seeing whether the result feels right. Yet within little more than a year, what initially sounded like a joke had become a mainstream software-development practice; some practitioners already describe it as the dominant paradigm for AI-assisted development. ([martinfowler.com][3])
>
> We suggest that \emph{vibe research} deserves to be examined in the same spirit. To many academics, the idea that an AI system could act as a substantive research collaborator---still more, that it might appropriately be represented as an author---may initially sound unserious. But the recent history of vibe coding gives some reason to be cautious about treating that reaction as an argument. Our purpose is therefore analogous, in a modest way, to that of the experienced barrister in \emph{The Castle}: not to appeal to ``the vibe'', but to ask whether the intuition can be reformulated in terms precise enough for the research community to evaluate.

I think that's much better. It establishes our tone immediately: **yes, we know the phrase sounds ridiculous; that's part of the point.**

### 5. The mathematics evidence is potentially very important

And since these developments are happening *right now*, there is suddenly much stronger evidence than there was even a few months ago.

A few cases are especially clean.

Erdős Problem 728 is described in the resulting mathematical write-up as the first Erdős problem regarded as “fully resolved autonomously by an AI system.” ([arXiv][4]) More substantially, a May 2026 DeepMind paper reports an agent autonomously resolving **9 of 353 open Erdős problems**, as well as 44 OEIS conjectures. ([arXiv][5]) The Erdős Problems project itself now maintains a list of notable AI contributions, including problems where autonomous systems found genuinely new solutions or techniques. ([GitHub][6])

The planar unit-distance result is particularly striking: an OpenAI model found a construction overturning an 80-year-old Erdős conjecture; *Nature* described mathematicians as astonished, and the result came from essentially a single initial prompt. ([Nature][7])

Then in July came the Jacobian conjecture counterexample. The mathematics itself is now on very solid ground: the explicit counterexample has been independently analysed and subsequently generalised in an arXiv paper. ([arXiv][8]) I would, however, be careful about saying **the AI unaided disproved it**: the provenance of the discovery is less well documented than the correctness of the counterexample itself. A good formulation is that Levent Alpöge credited Claude Fable with an important role in finding it; the result itself has since been confirmed. ([Monash Lens][9])

And the Riemann-zeta result may be the cleanest recent example of all. On August 10, Anthropic reported that an unreleased research Claude, working in Claude Code, increased the proven lower bound for zeros on the critical line from 41.6% to about 67.2%; the result was checked by mathematicians and formally verified in Lean. ([T.co][10]) That is not the Riemann Hypothesis, of course, but it is a serious new result in analytic number theory.

These are useful for our argument because they make one proposition increasingly difficult to maintain:

> **AI systems are merely instruments which execute intellectual content supplied by humans.**

We don't need to jump from that to “therefore AI authorship.” We can say something much more modest: current evidence includes cases in which AI systems generate nontrivial mathematical contributions whose content was not supplied in advance by a human. Any theory of research contribution now has to accommodate that empirical fact.

### The Go example is excellent, but I'd soften one part

I agree completely that Western academic audiences often underestimate what happened in Go. AlphaGo Zero/AlphaZero is particularly conceptually clean because it learned from scratch with no human game knowledge beyond the rules and reached superhuman performance. ([Google DeepMind][11]) Its successors developed strategies which professionals subsequently adopted; DeepMind documented new joseki such as the early 3--3 invasion causing a stir and entering professional play. ([Google DeepMind][12]) Empirical work on tens of thousands of professional games finds that professionals changed their standard opening patterns substantially after superhuman AI appeared. ([PubMed Central (PMC)][13])

I would **not** write that AI proved “most human-developed Go theory was wrong.” That's stronger than the evidence I found, and intriguingly a recent quantitative historical study actually argues that the total impact on *opening diversity* was smaller than common narratives sometimes suggest, while acknowledging that many famous sequences were modified or replaced. ([Cambridge University Press][14])

A safer and still impressive claim is:

> Go provides an earlier, unusually clear example of an AI system independently developing superhuman domain knowledge which subsequently forced human experts to revise a substantial body of accumulated professional theory, including established joseki and fuseki.

That is hard to dispute.

### Where I'd put this

I would **not** put all this into the last paragraph of the conclusion. It risks suddenly opening a huge new argument just as we're trying to close.

I'd add a short subsection after **“What Do the Examples Tell Us?”**, perhaps:

**`\subsection{Beyond Our Examples: AI as a Source of New Knowledge}`**

Only 3–5 paragraphs. Start with Go as the historical precursor, because it is conceptually exceptionally clean: AlphaZero received the rules, taught itself, surpassed humans, and human experts learned from it. Then one compact paragraph on the 2026 mathematics sequence: Erdős problems → unit distances → Jacobian → zeta.

Then the payoff:

> These cases do not settle questions of authorship or responsibility. They establish a narrower empirical point. It is no longer safe to assume, as part of the definition of AI-assisted intellectual work, that substantive ideas necessarily originate with the human participant.

That would lead beautifully back into the governance section. Policies designed around a **human-originates / AI-generates** ontology are not merely theoretically debatable; events are starting to falsify the descriptive assumption on which that ontology rests.

So I see the paper acquiring an even nicer arc:

**The Castle joke → vibe coding joke that became serious → define vibe research → accountability/provenance → our three concrete demonstrations → independent evidence from Go and frontier mathematics that AI intellectual contribution is real → why governance still describes things differently → institutional timescales → prevalence → conclusion.**

And I think the *Castle* motif should appear only twice: at the beginning, and perhaps a very light callback in the conclusion. Something like: **“The question is no longer whether there is a vibe, but whether our formal categories describe it adequately.”** Though possibly Denuto would tell us that's dangerously close to over-lawyering the case.
