### VR-E0061

**MANNY**

[Version 12]

**AI**

Yes — I think your restructuring is a substantial improvement. The paper now announces three candidate explanations in the abstract and then addresses them sequentially: responsibility/provenance, substantive results, and institutional constraints.  That gives it much more of the feeling of a single argument rather than a collection of related observations.

I also like the compression in the examples. The C-LARA-2 → Voice Mode → Grok progression now feels purposeful, and the Grok materials being actually present in C-LARA-2 rather than prospectively so makes the whole provenance argument cleaner.

There are, however, a few things I think we should definitely fix before showing it around.

The most important is in the **Conclusion**. I don't think we should say that *vibe research* is “a more neutral and better-defined term equivalent to the emotionally loaded phrase ‘AI authorship’.” They aren't equivalent. Vibe research is a **method of doing research**; AI authorship is one possible **way of representing a contribution** arising from that method. Someone could do vibe research and not list the AI as an author; conversely, an AI might conceivably earn authorship-level attribution without the workflow fitting our definition of vibe research. This was one of the conceptual distinctions we had worked quite hard to establish earlier.

I'd say something closer to:

> We have considered the idea of applying the methods of vibe coding to academic research, and suggested the term ``vibe research'' for this practice. Framing the issue in this way also provides a less emotionally loaded context in which to examine the related question of AI authorship.

Closely related: the conclusion introduces **“operational responsibility”**, but the body of the paper has carefully established **institutional accountability**, **intellectual accountability**, and **reliable provenance**. I think “operational responsibility” should disappear. It creates a new concept just when we're supposed to be consolidating the argument. Also, institutional accountability is not “essentially, ability to stand for legal consequences”; it includes retractions, sanctions, employment consequences, institutional answerability, etc. The body currently gets this distinction right.

So the conclusion should simply reuse our established terminology:

> We distinguish institutional accountability from intellectual accountability, and argue that for many of the questions at issue the operationally useful requirement is reliable intellectual provenance...

There is another important overstatement at the beginning of **Institutional Disallowance of Vibe Research**. At present it says that most professional bodies “simply do not allow AI authorship, vibe research, and associated activities.” I don't think we've established that, and indeed ACL's ambiguity is one of our arguments. What we can establish is that **AI authorship is widely disallowed**, and that sustained agentic research workflows can fit poorly or ambiguously into existing permitted-use taxonomies. The latter is subtler and more interesting. The existing ACL discussion itself is admirably careful about that.

Perhaps:

> A third issue is institutional. AI authorship is currently disallowed by many major academic bodies, while sustained agentic workflows such as those described here may fit only awkwardly into existing policies on permitted AI assistance.

That then gives us the right question: why can software teams change methodology directly while academic publication has to proceed through governance?

I also think the **mathematics section is a good addition**, but its framing can be slightly less defensive. “A determined sceptic might still respond...” makes it sound as though we're arguing with an imaginary hostile reviewer. I'd instead make it another straightforward empirical question:

> The preceding examples establish feasibility, but leave open a second question: can AI-mediated research produce results which the relevant research community regards as genuinely non-trivial? Recent developments in mathematics provide unusually clear evidence that it can.

That seems stronger because we don't need the sceptic at all.

And near the end of that argument I'd avoid:

> “teams of this kind have now succeeded in proving many highly non-trivial new theorems.”

Some examples are counterexamples, constructions, or improved bounds rather than “new theorems” in the ordinary sense, and they don't all instantiate exactly the same human–AI team structure. Better:

> Frontier AI systems have now made substantive contributions to multiple mathematical results which expert mathematicians regard as highly non-trivial, in some cases with minimal human input beyond specification of the problem.

That says exactly what our evidence supports.

### The three-objection structure can be sharpened slightly

I really like the new abstract structure, but I'd state the three candidate explanations in their strongest reasonable forms rather than slightly caricaturing them. At present the first says vibe research “requires the AI to take responsibility for its work, which it cannot do,” and the second says it “does not in practice generate any non-trivial results.”

Maybe:

> We consider three possible explanations. First, research collaboration may require forms of responsibility or accountability which AI systems cannot assume. Second, even if vibe research is technically possible, AI contributions may be too limited or trivial for the distinction to matter in practice. Third, academic institutions may simply not currently provide a place for this form of collaboration within their established governance structures.

Then “we argue that each explanation is less straightforward than it initially appears.” I prefer that to “none ... are convincing,” because the third one in particular **is** partly convincing: governance inertia really does explain something. What we're rejecting is not its explanatory power but the implication that current governance settles the substantive question.

### A few smaller things

I like **“Real Examples of Tracking Provenance”** conceptually, though perhaps `Three Examples of Verifiable Provenance` would sound a little less informal and directly state what they demonstrate.

`Non-Triviality of Vibe Research Results: Mathematics` could become **“Can Vibe Research Produce Non-Trivial Results? Evidence from Mathematics”**. That makes the three-section logic explicit.

`Institutional Disallowance of Vibe Research` could similarly become **“Institutional Constraints on Vibe Research”**. “Disallowance” is stronger than our evidence and sounds slightly prosecutorial.

There are also just a handful of mechanical slips: “multiple recent example” → “examples”; “verfiably” → “verifiably”; a missing full stop after “higher mathematics”; “academic generally” → “academics generally”; “better-defeined”; and a comma where there should be a full stop after “AI authorship” in the first conclusion paragraph.

My overall reaction, though, is very positive. The **compression was right**. In particular, the paper now has a simple high-level story a colleague could repeat after one reading:

> Vibe coding and vibe research look structurally similar. Why is one accepted and the other not? Perhaps because AI can't be accountable, perhaps because its research contribution isn't serious, or perhaps because academic governance can't yet accommodate it. We examine all three.

That is much easier to grasp, and the detailed provenance and ACL arguments now hang off a clear central structure instead of competing to define it. I think one careful revision dealing with the conceptual points above would put it very close to the version I'd be happy to send Cathy, Rina and Belinda.
